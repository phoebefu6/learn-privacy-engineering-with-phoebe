Noise with a guarantee

Four sessions of k, l and t all ended the same way: the protection depends on which columns you declared and what the outsider happens to know. Differential privacy makes a different kind of promise. It is a property of the release process, it holds whatever the outsider knows, and its price is a number you choose. Tonight: the definition in plain words, sensitivity, the Laplace mechanism, and a noisy table of real census counts that you release yourself.

Ravi Menon, the data engineer, could publish the true counts. But a count table can still give a person away: compare two tables, one with you and one without, and the difference is you. Part 1 · covers the definition

Two worlds, one person apart, nearly the same answers

5 min live

Picture two databases that are identical except for one person: in world A you are in the file, in world B you are not. Run the same release process on both. If the process is differentially private, the answers it can produce come out with almost the same probabilities in both worlds, so nobody looking at the output can tell which world they are in, and so cannot tell whether you are in the data. The word "almost" is made exact by one number, epsilon.

LiveThe definition, in one sentence3 min▶

The formal version. Dwork and Roth (2014), Definition 2.4: a mechanism M is (epsilon, delta)-differentially private if, for any two databases that differ in one person and any set S of outputs, the probability that M lands in S on one database is at most exp(epsilon) times the probability on the other, plus delta. With delta = 0, M is epsilon-differentially private, the version tonight uses.

The sentence for a DPO. "Whatever is published, it would have been almost as likely to be published if any one person had not been in the data; epsilon says how close 'almost' is." Small epsilon means the two worlds are nearly indistinguishable and the answers are noisy. Large epsilon means the answers are sharp and the two worlds are easier to tell apart.

Read the figure. The plum curve is the spread of answers the mechanism can give with you in the file, the dashed curve without you. They are shifted by exactly one person. At epsilon 1 their heights differ by a factor of at most exp(1), about 2.72, at every point on the axis.

LiveA property of the mechanism, not the file2 min▶

k is about the file. In Engineer session 4 the linkage attack broke a k = 5 release the moment one undeclared column, workclass, was published exact: k still printed 5, and single matches went from 0 to 22. k is only as good as your guess about which columns an outsider can know.

Epsilon is about the process. The definition says nothing about what the outsider knows. It compares the two worlds for every possible output, so it holds against an outsider who knows every other row in the file, a voter roll, a social media profile, or anything else. That is the guarantee in "noise with a guarantee", and it is why the definition is stated about M, never about the table M produced.

What it does not promise. It bounds what the output reveals about whether one person took part. It does not stop the release from revealing true facts about groups: if the table shows that degree holders earn more, that is the point of the table, not a leak.

Self-studyWhere the idea came from3 min▶

2006. Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith published the calibrated-noise idea in Lecture Notes in Computer Science (2006, pp 265-284). Their own name for the guarantee is epsilon-indistinguishability, and they show that adding noise of scale sensitivity / epsilon achieves it. The name "differential privacy" and the definition used tonight are from Dwork and Roth's monograph, The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy (Foundations and Trends in Theoretical Computer Science, 2014).

Where PDPC stands. Singapore's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) mentions differential privacy in Annex C as one of the "alternative methods" that "have also emerged", with a one-sentence description in a footnote that names a privacy budget. It does not teach it; the techniques it does teach are k-anonymity based. Whether a differentially private release is still personal data under the PDPA is a legal question: PDPA and DNC ↗ owns it.

Real world

The background knowledge nobody declared. When Netflix released movie ratings for its 500,000-subscriber Prize dataset, Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008) used public IMDb reviews as background knowledge and identified the Netflix records of known users; with 8 ratings and approximate dates, 99% of records could be uniquely identified. No anonymisation review had listed "IMDb reviews" as a quasi-identifier. A guarantee that holds whatever the outsider knows is the answer to exactly that failure.

Part 2 · covers sensitivity

Sensitivity: how far one person can move the answer

5 min live

The noise has to hide one person, so its size depends on how much one person can change the answer. That amount is the sensitivity of the query. Get it right and the guarantee holds; get it wrong and the noise is too small for the one person who matters.

LiveSensitivity of a count, and of a sum3 min▶

The definition. Dwork and Roth, Definition 3.1: the l1-sensitivity of a query is the largest change in its answer, summed over every output number, between any two databases that differ in one person. It is a property of the query, worked out before you look at the data.

A count has sensitivity 1. Brightwater's query is the number of people over 50K at each of 16 education levels. Each person has exactly one education level, so adding or removing one person changes one of the 16 counts by 1 and leaves the other 15 alone. The whole table has sensitivity 1.

A sum of incomes has no bound. UCI Adult has a capital gain column. If the partner asked for total capital gain by education level, one person with an enormous gain could move a total by any amount, so no finite noise hides them. The fix is clipping: before summing, cap every person's value at a limit C you set in advance (values above C count as C). Now one person moves a total by at most C, the sensitivity is C, and the noise can be sized to it. Clipping changes the answer for the people above the cap; that bias is the price, and it goes on the release record.

Self-studyWho counts as "one person"2 min▶

The census extract has one row per person, so "differ in one person" and "differ in one row" are the same thing here. In event data they are not: a customer with 300 transactions is 300 rows. If the sensitivity is worked out per row, the guarantee protects one transaction, not the customer. Engineer session 6 takes this up as the unit of privacy; for tonight, check that one row is one person before you write "sensitivity 1".

Constructed Brightwater example

Ravi's second query. After the count table, the same partner asks for mean hours worked by education level. Hours worked per week has a natural upper limit he could use as the clip, but a mean also divides by a count that is itself private. He writes both down as open questions for the release record rather than guessing a sensitivity. The habit is the point: no sensitivity, no release.

Part 3 · covers the Laplace mechanism

Laplace noise, sized to sensitivity over epsilon

5 min live

The recipe fits on one line. Draw noise from a Laplace distribution with scale b = sensitivity / epsilon, add it to the true answer, publish the sum. For Brightwater's count table the sensitivity is 1, so b = 1 / epsilon. The noise has the same size on every count, which is the whole story of what it costs: a count of 2,221 barely notices, a count of 0 is swamped.

LiveThe mechanism and its numbers4 min▶

The theorem. Dwork and Roth, Theorem 3.6: adding noise drawn from Lap(sensitivity / epsilon) to each output preserves (epsilon, 0)-differential privacy. The Laplace distribution with scale b is a sharp peak at zero with exponential tails; its variance is 2 b2, so its standard deviation is about 1.41 b. The 2006 paper loosely calls b itself the standard deviation; teach b as the scale.

Brightwater's table, measured. The 16 true counts of over-50K earners, Preschool 0 through Doctorate 306, total 7,841. With the seeded draw the widget uses:

  • Epsilon 1, b = 1: mean absolute error 0.94 people per count; Preschool released as -3.8.
  • Epsilon 0.1, b = 10: mean absolute error 9.42; Preschool released as -37.5.
  • Epsilon 2, b = 0.5: mean absolute error 0.47.

Same error, different damage. An error of about one person is nothing on Bachelors' 2,221 and a lot on 1st-4th's 6. At epsilon 0.1 the small counts are pure noise: a released -37.5 for a true 0 says nothing about Preschool at all. Before choosing epsilon, ask which counts the partner needs to be right; if they are the small ones, noise is the wrong tool, or the bins must be merged first.

Self-studyPost-processing is free2 min▶

A released -3.8 people looks broken. Round it, clamp it at 0, make the counts whole numbers: none of this weakens the guarantee, because anything you compute from a differentially private output without going back to the data is just as private. This is a well-known property of differential privacy, and Dwork and Roth discuss it as post-processing. Two cautions. First, post-processing must not look at the true data: "clamp at 0 unless the true count is positive" leaks. Second, clamping changes the error: every negative becomes 0, which nudges small counts upward on average. Publish the table and say which post-processing you applied.

Real world

A budget printed in a press release. For the 2020 Census redistricting data product, the US Census Bureau published "a total privacy-loss budget" of epsilon 19.61: 17.14 for the persons file and 2.47 for the housing unit data (press release CB21-CN.42, 9 June 2021). The release names the TopDown Algorithm; the Bureau's move to differential privacy for 2020 is widely reported. The figure covers the redistricting product only. Engineer session 6 picks up what it means to spend a budget like that across many tables.

★ Step 4 · numpy, seededimport numpy as np levels = ["Preschool", "1st-4th", "5th-6th", "7th-8th", "9th", "10th", "11th", "12th", "HS-grad", "Some-college", "Assoc-voc", "Assoc-acdm", "Bachelors", "Masters", "Prof-school", "Doctorate"] true = np.array([0, 6, 16, 40, 27, 62, 60, 33, 1675, 1387, 361, 265, 2221, 959, 423, 306]) print("total", true.sum()) for eps in (1.0, 0.1, 2.0): noise = np.random.default_rng(7).laplace(0, 1 / eps, 16) noisy = true + noise mae = np.abs(noisy - true).mean() print(f"epsilon {eps}: scale b {1 / eps:g}, MAE {mae:.2f}, " f"Preschool {noisy[0]:.1f}, Bachelors {noisy[12]:.1f}") # output of a real run: # total 7841 # epsilon 1.0: scale b 1, MAE 0.87, Preschool 0.3, Bachelors 2220.3 # epsilon 0.1: scale b 10, MAE 8.69, Preschool 2.9, Bachelors 2214.3 # epsilon 2.0: scale b 0.5, MAE 0.43, Preschool 0.1, Bachelors 2220.7

Read the pattern, not the digits. Each draw reuses seed 7, so the same underlying noise is stretched by 1 / epsilon: the epsilon 0.1 errors are exactly ten times the epsilon 1 errors. The widget's 0.94 and numpy's 0.87 are two draws at the same scale.

Never re-roll the noise. If the first draw looks bad and you draw again, then publish the nicer one, you have answered the query twice and chosen with the true data in view. Draw once, publish what you drew, and record the seed policy.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does epsilon bound in the definition of differential privacy?

The definition compares the two worlds, with and without one person: every output's probability can differ by at most a factor exp(epsilon). It is a statement about the mechanism, not about classes in a file.

2 · What is the sensitivity of Brightwater's table of 16 counts of over-50K earners by education level?

Adding or removing one person changes one count by 1 and leaves the other 15 unchanged. Clipping is needed for sums of unbounded values, not for counts.

3 · At epsilon 1 the mean error is 0.94 people per count. Why does that ruin the Preschool and 1st-4th counts but not Bachelors?

The Laplace scale depends only on sensitivity and epsilon, never on the true value. About one person of error is nothing on 2,221 and decisive on 0 or 6.
