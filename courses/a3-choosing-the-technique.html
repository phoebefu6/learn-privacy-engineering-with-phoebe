Choosing the technique

"Just mask it" is a technique choice made before anyone asked what the release is for. Tonight: the techniques Singapore's regulator lists and what each one does to a row, the three families a leader actually chooses between, what each one costs when measured on real census rows, and a simple tree that matches the tool to the thing leaving the building, with the US Census Bureau's published budget as a real example of choosing noise at scale.

Part 0

Where we start

Leader session 1 put a number on the gap between pseudonymised and anonymised. Leader session 2 showed that the route, open data, named partners or an enclave, changes how big the gap is allowed to be. Tonight is the toolbox. Every technique closes the gap by taking something away from the people who receive the data: detail, accuracy, or the real rows themselves. A leader's job is not to pick the cleverest tool; it is to pick the one whose bill the recipient can afford to pay. Brightwater Social Research Institute and its three requests are constructed and labelled. Every cost on this page is measured on real rows: 32,561 people from the UCI Adult extract of the 1994 US Census.

Part 1 · covers PDPC's technique list

Eight techniques, sorted by what they do to a row

5 min live

PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) lists seven techniques in its Annex A: record suppression, character masking, pseudonymisation, generalisation, swapping, data perturbation and data aggregation. An eighth, attribute suppression, is defined in Step 3 and treated in Annex B. Eight names are hard to hold in a meeting. Sort them by what they do to a row and there are only five moves: remove something, coarsen it, scramble or replace it, add noise to it, or summarise many rows into one number.

LiveWhat each move takes away3 min▶

Remove takes whole rows (record suppression), whole columns (attribute suppression) or part of a value (character masking). It is exact about what is left and blunt about what is lost.

Coarsen turns exact values into bands: age 37 becomes 30-39, sixteen education levels become five. Every row stays; every row gets vaguer.

Swap or replace moves values between rows (swapping) or replaces an identifier with a token (pseudonymisation). Pseudonymisation is a replacement, not a protection: Leader session 1 showed that the columns around a token can still lead back.

Add noise nudges values or results by a random amount (data perturbation). Summarise releases counts and averages instead of rows (data aggregation). These two are where calibrated noise on counts, Part 2's second family, comes from.

Self-studyWhere the technique sits in PDPC's five steps2 min▶

The guide sets out five steps: Step 1, know your data; Step 2, de-identify your data; Step 3, apply anonymisation techniques; Step 4, compute your risk; Step 5, manage your re-identification and disclosure risks. Two things follow for a leader. First, removing direct identifiers is Step 2, and the guide calls de-identification only the first step of anonymisation; choosing a technique is Step 3. Second, a technique is never the end: Step 4 measures what it achieved, and Step 5 manages what is left. Leader session 4 covers the sign-off that closes Steps 4 and 5.

Self-studyMasking is for identifiers, not for the facts around them2 min▶

Character masking hides part of a value, typically an identity number or a phone number. It is a sensible move on a direct identifier and almost no move at all on the quasi-identifiers, because nobody masks an age or a country of birth. On Brightwater's rows, the six ordinary columns alone leave 17.18% of people unique whatever happens to the identity number. Internal masking for staff who see production data is a different problem with its own tools; Data Access Control ↗ owns it.

Real world

"Can we just mask it?" Ravi Menon's first proposal for the partner extract was to mask the hashed identity numbers down to their first two digits. Lim Su-Ann pointed at the other columns: age, sex, race, marital status, education and country of birth, all exact. Masking moved nothing that mattered; 5,594 people were still alone on those six facts. The right question was not "which technique?" but "what does each partner need to do with the file?" Constructed Brightwater example; the numbers are measured on the real rows.

Part 2 · covers three families and their costs

Three families, three different bills

5 min live

In practice a release ends up in one of three families. Generalise and suppress until every combination is shared by at least k people. Release only counts, with calibrated noise added so that no single person changes the answer much. Or release synthetic rows that imitate the real ones. Each family pays its bill in a different currency, which is why their numbers can never be put on one chart without lying. Here is what each one cost on Brightwater's real rows, as the Engineer track measured it.

LiveGeneralise and suppress: the bill is paid in detail and in people2 min▶

What it buys. At the heaviest setting the Engineer track measured, rung 6 (age in 20-year bands, education as degree yes or no, k 10), not one of 1,000 people an outsider knows six facts about can be matched to a single row.

What it costs. Precision falls to 51%. Precision is the Release Desk's rule of thumb: 1 minus the average generalisation height, so 51% means the columns keep, on average, about half their detail. And 345 rows, 1.06% of the file, are withheld because their combination is still too rare.

What it does not stop. The protection holds only for the columns the release record lists. Engineer session 4 shows what happens when one more column ships exact without being declared: on the k 5 file, single matches go from 0 to 22 while k still reads 5. The rows are still rows, so anyone who can see them can try.

LiveNoise on counts: the bill is paid in error2 min▶

What it buys. Differential privacy adds random noise, sized by a setting called epsilon, so that adding or removing any one person barely changes what comes out. The guarantee is about the method, not about one particular file, and it holds whatever the outsider already knows.

What it costs. On sixteen counts of high earners by education level, epsilon 1 gives a mean absolute error of 0.94 people per count. Epsilon 0.1, ten times stronger, gives 9.42. The noise is the same size for every count, so it is invisible on 2,221 Bachelors and fatal on small cells: the Preschool count of 0 was released as -3.8.

What it does not stop. Asking too many questions. Every query spends part of a budget, and a budget split across ten queries makes each one as noisy as epsilon 0.1. Engineer session 6 spends one. There is no agreed "safe" epsilon; that disagreement is real and not resolved here.

LiveSynthetic rows: the bill depends on the generator1 min▶

Generator one copies nothing and keeps nothing. A generator that draws each column independently keeps every column's mix and destroys the relationships between them. On the real rows, men are 19.6 points more likely than women to earn over 50K; in its synthetic rows, 0.2. A researcher studying the pay gap gets a file that says there is none.

Generator two keeps everything by copying. A generator that memorises real rows keeps the gap (19.9) and reproduces 6,818 of the 13,496 real people who are unique in the file. Even the independent generator matches 2,183 of them exactly, by chance, which is why a copy count only means something against a chance baseline. Engineer session 7 runs both.

Self-studyThe disagreement on synthetic data3 min▶

The vendor position. A common sales claim is that synthetic data is anonymous by construction: no row is a real person, so nothing can be re-identified.

The research position. Stadler, Oprisanu and Troncoso ("Synthetic Data - Anonymisation Groundhog Day", USENIX Security 2022) tested generators against linkage, membership inference and attribute inference. Their finding: synthetic data either does not prevent the attacks or does not keep the data useful, giving no better tradeoff than traditional anonymisation, and the tradeoff is hard to predict. Their phrase: synthetic data is "far from the holy grail of privacy-preserving data publishing".

The regulator's position. PDPC's guide does not list synthetic data as a technique. It treats record-level synthetic data as a use case, created by heavily anonymising the original, and says it is not suitable for sophisticated purposes such as AI model training or data analytics. Generating synthetic data from scratch is outside the guide's scope.

What a leader does with that. Neither side is settled for every generator, so do not settle it by assertion. Approve synthetic rows only with a test result attached: copies against a chance baseline, and a check on the one relationship the user needs.

Self-studyWhere PDPC's guide stops1 min▶

The guide measures risk with k-anonymity. In a footnote it notes that homogeneity attacks can be addressed with the extensions l-diversity and t-closeness, and says those topics are outside its scope. In Annex C it says alternative methods such as differential privacy have also emerged. It teaches neither. Engineer sessions 3, 5 and 6 cover them from the research that defined them. A release built on them is not outside PDPC's thinking; it is past the point where the basic guide stops giving instructions.

Real world

Groundhog Day. Stadler and colleagues chose their title deliberately: the promise that a new method makes data safe to share without loss has been made before, about every earlier method, and the same tradeoff came back each time. That is the posture to bring to any pitch that says a technique has no bill.

Part 3 · covers matching tool to release

Match the tool to what leaves the building

5 min live

Start from the output, not the technique. If what leaves is a table of counts, noise on the counts protects every person and costs only accuracy you can state. If what leaves is rows for analysis, generalise and suppress, or keep the rows in an enclave and let only results out. If what leaves is rows that only need to look real, for testing or a demo, synthetic rows can do it, as long as someone has tested them for copies. The largest example of the first branch is a national census.

LiveReading the tree in a meeting3 min▶

Counts or tables. When the user only ever needs totals, give them totals. Noise protects everyone in the table, including the people in the small cells that a generalised file would have had to withhold, and its cost is a number you can print beside the table. The price is that the owner has to accept "about 361", not "361".

Rows for analysis. A researcher fitting a model needs rows. Generalisation keeps rows but blurs exactly the columns a model leans on. When the analysis needs the fine detail, the honest answer is often not a file at all but an enclave: the rows stay inside, the researcher's code runs there, and only checked results leave. Leader session 2 set out that route.

Realistic rows for testing. A hackathon or a test system needs rows that behave like the real ones in shape, not in truth. That is the one job synthetic rows suit without argument, provided they were tested for copies. PDPC's guide makes the same split: record-level synthetic data for uses like testing, not for AI model training or data analytics.

Self-studyWhat the Census release says, and what it does not3 min▶

What it says. The US Census Bureau's press release CB21-CN.42, dated 9 June 2021, sets "a total privacy-loss budget for the redistricting data product" of epsilon 19.61, which includes 17.14 for the persons file and 2.47 for the housing unit data. It says the team will prepare the TopDown Algorithm for final testing.

What it does not say. The number covers the redistricting product only, not every 2020 Census output. The budget for the later demographic and housing characteristics file is not given here, and no primary figure for it is used on this page. The release itself does not use the words "differential privacy"; the Bureau's move to differential privacy for 2020 is widely reported, and the release's own terms are a privacy-loss budget and the TopDown Algorithm.

What a leader takes from it. A national statistics office chose a number, split it between two files, and published it. 19.61 is not a recommendation for anyone else's data: a budget only means something together with its unit of privacy and the queries it covers, which Leader session 4 puts on the sign-off.

Real world

A budget, written down. The most useful thing about the Census Bureau's 19.61 is not its size; it is that it exists in public, split by file, with a date and a release number. Most organisations that add noise to outputs cannot tell you their epsilon at all. When a team proposes noise on counts, ask for the number, the split and the queries it covers, the three things the Bureau put in one paragraph.

Build-along

A technique for each request

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Three requests have reached Lim Su-Ann's desk in the same week. Brightwater's communications team wants a public dashboard. One of the university partners wants rows for a regression. Dr Hana Seow has agreed to sponsor a student hackathon that wants "realistic data". One extract, three outputs, three different answers. Work through each, write down the tool, its measured cost and the one thing it does not protect against, then read the noise yourself in the widget before you brief the dashboard's owner.

Step 1 · Request A, the public dashboard. The communications team wants counts of people by education level and income band, on a public page. Nobody will ever see a row. Run it down the tree: the output is a table of counts, so the branch is noise on the counts. A public page has no agreement and no recipient list, so the protection has to live in the numbers themselves.

★ Step 1 · request Aoutput: counts by education x income band, public page branch: counts or tables -> noise on the counts why not rows: nobody needs a row; a row on a public page has no controls Brightwater: approve noise; size it in step 5

Step 2 · Request B, the partner regression. A university team wants row-level data to model income on age and education. The tree says generalise and suppress. Now read the cost against the purpose: rung 6, the setting that gets single matches to 0, puts age in 20-year bands and education into degree yes or no. Those are the two columns the regression is about.

★ Step 2 · request Boutput: rows, for a regression on age and education branch: rows for analysis -> generalise + suppress, or enclave rung 6 cost: precision 51%, 345 rows withheld, age 20y, degree yes/no conflict: the protection removes the detail the model needs Brightwater: offer the enclave first; a coarsened file second

Step 3 · Request C, the hackathon. Students want rows that look real so they can build and test tools over a weekend. Nobody is publishing findings from them. This is the branch synthetic rows suit, and the only one: PDPC's guide says record-level synthetic data is not suitable for AI model training or data analytics. The condition is a test for copies before a single row leaves.

★ Step 3 · request Coutput: realistic rows for a weekend of building and testing branch: realistic rows -> synthetic, tested for copies test: exact copies of real unique people vs a chance baseline (chance alone gives 2,183 of 13,496; memorising gives 6,818) Brightwater: approve synthetic, with the copy test attached

Step 4 · Write the table. One row per request: the tool, the measured cost, and the one thing it does not protect against. The third column is the one requesters skip and the one that keeps you honest. Each line should be readable by the person who asked.

Step 5 · Read the noise before you brief request A's owner. Open the widget below. At its default, epsilon 1 and one query, read the mean absolute error across the sixteen education counts and look at the Preschool bar. Then move epsilon to 0.1 and read both again. The widget prints 0.94 and -3.8 at epsilon 1, and 9.42 and -37.5 at epsilon 0.1. Those two pairs are your brief.

★ Step 5 · the brief for request AAt epsilon 1, each published count is off by about 0.94 people on average. At epsilon 0.1 it is off by about 9.42; the protection is stronger, the table is vaguer. Small cells suffer most: a true 0 was released as -3.8 (epsilon 1) and -37.5 (epsilon 0.1). Clamping negatives to 0 afterwards costs no privacy. Decision needed from you: which epsilon, and how many queries it must cover: ______

Give every requester the third column. "You get noisy counts, about one person of error each, and the budget runs out if you ask for fifty breakdowns" is an answer a communications team can plan around. A tool without its limit is a promise you will have to break later.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · How does PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) treat record-level synthetic data?

Synthetic data appears as a use case, not a technique, and the guide limits it. Generating it from scratch is outside the guide's scope. The "anonymous by construction" claim is the vendor position that Stadler et al. tested and disputed.

2 · The communications team wants a public dashboard of counts by education level. Which family suits it best?

When only totals leave, release totals. Noise protects every person, including those in small cells, and its cost is printable: about 0.94 people per count at epsilon 1 on Brightwater's sixteen counts.

3 · An engineer reports that the heavily generalised file has a precision of 51%. What does that mean?

Precision is 1 minus the average generalisation height, a usefulness measure, not a risk measure. At that setting only 345 rows, 1.06%, were withheld, and 0 of 1,000 known people matched a single row.
