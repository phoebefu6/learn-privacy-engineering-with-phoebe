The linkage attack

Every measure so far has been a property of the release: k, l, t, the unique share. Tonight you stop measuring the file and attack it. An outsider holds a table of real people with six facts each, generalises it the way the release did, joins on the shared key and counts who comes back alone. It is the test closest to what actually happens to a released file, and it is the one that catches the column nobody declared.

Part 1 · covers the join

The outsider joins on the release's own key

5 min live

The attacker does not need anything clever. They hold a table of people they know: name, plus age, sex, race, marital status, education and country of birth, exact. They read the release's documentation, generalise their own table to the same levels, and join on the six generalised columns. Each person they know lands in one class of the release. If that class has c rows, they have a 1 in c chance of pointing at the right one. When c is 1, the join is a name.

LiveWhat the outsider holds, and why it is realistic3 min▶

A table, not a hunch. A voter roll, a staff directory, a membership list, a scraped set of public profiles. Each row is a named person with a few ordinary facts. The attack in the widget gives the outsider 1,000 real rows from the file with all six facts exact: a strong outsider, and a fair one to test against, because a release that survives them survives weaker ones.

The release tells them how to generalise. A release has to document its bands and groups, or the recipient cannot use it. "Age in 10-year bands, education in five levels" is a recipe the outsider follows on their own table in one line of code. Generalisation does not hide the mapping; it only makes the classes bigger.

The target is in the file. The outsider's people are rows of the release, so the class they land in always contains them. That is the prosecutor case: the attacker knows the person is in the data and asks which row is theirs. The chance of picking the right row is 1 / c, and a class of 1 makes it certain.

Self-studyIdentity disclosure, and where it sits in PDPC's steps2 min▶

PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) works in five steps: know your data, de-identify, apply techniques, compute your risk, manage the re-identification and disclosure risks. Step 5 names identity disclosure, a record tied to a person, beside attribute and inference disclosure. The linkage attack is the most direct way to compute identity disclosure for a specific outsider: count the people who come back alone. Whether a file that passes is still personal data is a legal question; PDPA and DNC ↗ owns it. This is engineering, not legal advice.

Real world

Twenty dollars and a join. The Massachusetts Group Insurance Commission held hospital records for about 135,000 state employees and their families, with names removed, and gave a copy to researchers. Latanya Sweeney bought the Cambridge voter list for twenty dollars. Six people on it had the governor's birth date, three of them were men, and only one lived in his 5-digit ZIP code. Joining the two tables on ZIP, birth date and sex returned William Weld's records: a class of 1, reached with exactly the join on this page (Sweeney 2002).

Part 2 · covers prosecutor risk and the ladder

Count the matches rung by rung

5 min live

Run the same 1,000 known people against each rung of the ladder and read two numbers. Single-row matches count the people whose class has exactly one row: named outright. Expected re-identifications add up 1 / class size over all 1,000: the number of people a guesser would name correctly on average if they picked one row from each class. The first number reaches zero. The second does not.

LiveReading the two numbers3 min▶

Single-row matches: 175, 85, 6, 0, 0, 0. At rung 1, pseudonymised only, 175 of the 1,000 known people are alone in the release and named outright. Banding age halves it to 85. Coarsening five columns leaves 6. From rung 4 on, every class under k is suppressed, so no published class has one row and the count is 0 by construction.

Expected re-identifications: 272.5, 129.6, 29.0, 16.7, 10.8, 4.4. Each known person contributes 1 / c, the chance a guesser picks their row. A person in a class of 1 adds 1; a class of 5 adds 0.2; a class of 10 adds 0.1. Adding up the 1,000 gives the number of people the outsider would name correctly on average. At rung 1 it is higher than the single count because people in classes of 2 and 3 add up too.

Occupation revealed: 201, 97, 10, 0. The attack does not need a single match to hurt. If everyone in the class shares an occupation, the outsider learns the job without knowing which row is the person: the homogeneity problem of Engineer session 3, now counted for real targets.

Self-studyWhy the expected count never reaches zero3 min▶

Every published person adds something. A known person whose row is published sits in a class of some size c, and adds 1 / c. With k of 10 each adds at most 0.1, which is why rung 5 and rung 6 are small, but a positive number times any number of people is never zero. The only people who add nothing are the suppressed ones, whose rows are not in the file.

So zero means an empty release. To drive expected re-identifications to 0 you would suppress every row. That is the honest shape of the problem: generalisation and suppression lower the risk and never remove it. A release record states the residual number and who accepted it, rather than claiming none.

The bound is simple. With k achieved, the expected count can be at most the number of published targets divided by k. At rung 6, k 10, that bound is at most 100 and the real number is 4.4, because most of the 1,000 sit in classes much bigger than 10.

Real world

Netflix, with IMDb as the outsider's table. The Netflix Prize data held ratings from about 500,000 subscribers with names replaced. Narayanan and Shmatikov used public IMDb reviews as auxiliary data and identified the Netflix records of known users. Their numbers: with 8 movie ratings, of which 2 may be wrong, and dates within 14 days, 99% of records could be uniquely identified, and the auxiliary information needed was 2 to 8 ratings per user. Movie ratings were never declared a quasi-identifier. The join did not care.

Part 3 · covers the forgotten column and two releases

A column nobody declared splits the classes again

5 min live

k is computed over the columns you declare as quasi-identifiers. If a column is published but never declared, k is computed over the wrong key, and the printed k is a statement about a file the outsider is not attacking. Rung 4 with workclass published exact and undeclared still prints k = 5. The outsider joins on seven columns, not six, and single-row matches go from 0 back to 22.

LiveThe break, in four numbers3 min▶

k still prints 5. The check ran on the six declared columns, and on those six every published class still has at least 5 rows. Nothing in the k calculation knows workclass is in the file.

Single-row matches 0 to 22. The outsider knows workclass too, so they join on seven columns. Classes of 5 split into smaller classes, and 22 of the 1,000 known people come back alone.

Expected re-identifications 16.7 to 73.5, occupation revealed 0 to 89. The smaller classes add more 1 / c each, and many of them now hold one occupation. Workclass is not a sensitive column and not an exotic one: on the six exact columns it takes the share of people alone from 17.18% to 27.78%.

The fix is a declaration, not a technique. Every column that leaves the building goes into the quasi-identifier list unless someone writes down why no outsider could know it. Then k, l, t and the attack are all computed over the key the outsider will actually use.

Self-studyTwo releases of the same people3 min▶

A constructed explanation, because no source on this page measured it. Suppose release 1 is the rung 4 file and release 2, of the same people, carries age band, sex, workclass and hours. Neither release has to be weak on its own. An outsider who holds both looks up a known person in each, keeps only the rows consistent with both, and in effect attacks one release that declares the union of their columns. The forgotten-column break is the closest computed stand-in: it is rung 4 with one more column the first check never saw.

How well two real releases join depends on whether rows can be matched across them, which depends on shared columns, record order and what else was published, so the computed 22 is an illustration of the direction, not a measurement of a two-release case. The engineering rule does not depend on the exact number: a release is checked against everything already released about the same people, not alone.

Real world

Constructed Brightwater example: the second partner. Three months after the rung 4 file went out, a second university team asks Ravi for workclass and hours, for a study of working patterns. Each request looks modest. Lim Su-Ann asks him to run the attack on the union of both releases before sending anything. Run with workclass added, the desk reports 22 single-row matches where the first release reported 0, and Ravi's answer becomes a redesign of the second release rather than a send.

★ Step 4 · pandas, the join at rung 3import pandas as pd cols = ["age", "workclass", "fnlwgt", "education", "education_num", "marital", "occupation", "relationship", "race", "sex", "capital_gain", "capital_loss", "hours", "country", "income"] df = pd.read_csv("adult.data", names=cols, skipinitialspace=True) edu_order = ["Preschool", "1st-4th", "5th-6th", "7th-8th", "9th", "10th", "11th", "12th", "HS-grad", "Some-college", "Assoc-voc", "Assoc-acdm", "Bachelors", "Masters", "Prof-school", "Doctorate"] def edu5(e): i = edu_order.index(e) if i <= 7: return "No high school diploma" if i == 8: return "High school" if i <= 11: return "Some college or associate" if i == 12: return "Bachelors" return "Postgraduate" def marital3(m): if m.startswith("Married"): return "Married" if m == "Never-married": return "Never married" return "Previously married" def country3(c): if c == "United-States": return "United States" if c == "?": return "Unknown" return "Outside US" def rung3(t): # the release's own mapping, applied to any table return pd.DataFrame({ "age": (t["age"] // 10 * 10).astype(str) + "-" + (t["age"] // 10 * 10 + 9).astype(str), "sex": t["sex"], "race": t["race"].where(t["race"] == "White", "Not white"), "marital": t["marital"].map(marital3), "education": t["education"].map(edu5), "country": t["country"].map(country3), }, index=t.index) key = ["age", "sex", "race", "marital", "education", "country"] release = rung3(df) # what Brightwater published outsider = rung3(df.sample(1000, random_state=7)) # the outsider's 1,000, generalised the same way outsider["target"] = outsider.index joined = outsider.merge(release, on=key, how="left") # every release row each target could be size = joined.groupby("target").size() # class size per target print("targets: ", len(size)) print("single matches: ", int((size == 1).sum())) print("expected re-ids: ", round((1 / size).sum(), 1)) print("release classes: ", release.groupby(key).ngroups) # output with random_state=7 (pandas 2.1.4): # targets: 1000 # single matches: 3 # expected re-ids: 23.1 # release classes: 872

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · An outsider joins a known person to the release and lands in a class of 4. What does 1 / 4 mean?

The target is one of the four rows, so a guess is right one time in four. Adding 1 / class size over all known people gives the expected re-identifications.

2 · From rung 4 on, single-row matches are 0, yet expected re-identifications are 16.7, 10.8 and 4.4. Why does the expected count never reach 0?

Suppression removes classes under k, so no class has one row, but every remaining person still adds a small positive amount. Zero would mean publishing nobody.

3 · At rung 4, publishing workclass exact without declaring it leaves k printed at 5 but takes single-row matches from 0 to 22. What does that show?

The outsider joins on what is published, not on what is declared. Declaring every published column, and running the attack on the final column list, closes the gap.
