Pseudonymous is not anonymous

"We removed the names, so it is anonymous." It is the most common sentence in a data-sharing request and it is almost never true. Tonight: where Singapore's regulator draws the line between pseudonymised and anonymised data, why the test is about re-identification risk rather than which columns were deleted, how six ordinary facts single out more than one in six people on real census rows, and three famous releases that were undone by exactly that.

Part 1 · covers the PDPC test

The line is drawn by risk, not by columns

5 min live

Data moves along a line. Identified data names the person. Pseudonymised data has the names and ID numbers replaced, while someone, somewhere, still holds the key or the other columns that lead back. Anonymised data is data where re-identification is no longer a serious possibility. PDPC's guidelines put the test in those terms: data is not considered anonymised if there is a serious possibility that an individual could be re-identified, taking into account both the data and what the recipient has or is likely to have access to, and the safeguards around it.

LiveWhat the test asks, in plain words3 min▶

Not "did you delete the identifiers?" PDPC treats removing direct identifiers as a required step, and its Guide to Basic Anonymisation calls de-identification only the first step of anonymisation. The test is what is left.

"Could this person be re-identified, seriously?" The guidelines weigh the data together with other information the recipient has or is likely to have, and the measures and safeguards, or the lack of them. Their listed factors: how much the data was altered, how widely it is disclosed, what other information is available, and the recipients' ability and motivation to re-identify.

"Still true next year?" The guidelines note that the effectiveness of anonymisation may degrade over time and recommend periodic review. Anonymised is a state you maintain, not a label you stamp once.

Self-studyThe same distinction in the EU2 min▶

The GDPR defines pseudonymisation in Article 4(5) as processing so that data can no longer be attributed to a person without additional information kept separately, and Recital 26 says the principles of data protection should not apply to anonymous information. Pseudonymised data stays in scope; anonymous data falls outside it. How the two regimes compare, and what that means for a Singapore company with EU data, is covered in GDPR ↗. The engineering question, how much risk is left, is the same under both.

Real world

"We hashed the NRIC." Ravi Menon, Brightwater's data engineer, replaced every identity number with a hash before the first draft of the extract went to review. Lim Su-Ann, the DPO, asked one question: what else is in the file? Age, sex, race, marital status, education, country of birth, workclass, occupation, income. The hash took the file from the first station to the second. Everything in this track is about the gap to the third. Constructed Brightwater example; the pattern is the common one.

Part 2 · covers uniqueness on the rows

Ordinary facts single people out

5 min live

Re-identification rarely needs a secret. It needs a combination. On Brightwater's real rows, age alone leaves 2 people of 32,561 with nobody else of their age. Add sex, race, marital status, education and country of birth, six facts a colleague could know, and 5,594 people, 17.18%, share their combination with nobody in the file. Anyone who knows those six facts about one of them can find their row and read the rest.

LiveWhy it falls hardest on the few3 min▶

Rare combinations are empty. Six columns with 73, 2, 5, 7, 16 and 42 values allow 3,433,920 combinations, for 32,561 people. Most people sit in the common combinations; the rest are alone.

The alone are not random. 29,170 of the rows are US-born. The 2,808 born elsewhere, the small racial groups, the rare degrees, are the ones in classes of one. An unsafe release exposes minorities and migrants first.

A leader's number. Ask your engineers for the share of rows in a class of one, on the columns an outsider could know. It is one number, it takes minutes, and it ends the "we removed the names" conversation.

Self-studyThe 99.98% figure, and why it is a model2 min▶

Rocher, Hendrickx and de Montjoye (Nature Communications, 2019) fitted a statistical model that estimated 99.98% of Americans would be correctly re-identified in any dataset using 15 demographic attributes. It is widely quoted as a measurement. It is a model estimate, validated on many populations with good but imperfect accuracy, and the body of the paper states the figure for Massachusetts. Quote it as an estimate, and pair it with your own file's measured share.

Part 3 · covers releases that failed

Three releases that were undone

5 min live

Every famous re-identification has the same three parts: something was removed, something ordinary was left, and somebody outside already knew the ordinary thing. Learn the shape once and you will see it in every release request.

LiveWhat each one teaches a leader4 min▶

Massachusetts. The insurer released about 135,000 state employees' and families' records with names removed. Latanya Sweeney bought the Cambridge voter list for twenty dollars. Six people on it shared the governor's birth date, three were men, and he was the only one in his ZIP code. Lesson: the outsider's knowledge is often public and cheap.

AOL. Reporters from The New York Times followed the searches of user 4417749 to Thelma Arnold, a 62-year-old widow in Lilburn, Georgia. Lesson: any column about a person can identify, including free text nobody listed as a quasi-identifier.

Netflix. Narayanan and Shmatikov showed that 8 ratings, 2 of which could be wrong, with dates known to within 14 days, single out 99% of records, and that public IMDb reviews were enough to find known users. Lesson: sparse, rich data is identifying even when every single value is common.

Self-studyWhy "nobody would bother" is not a defence2 min▶

PDPC's guidelines describe a motivated intruder test as good practice: assume someone reasonably competent and motivated, with the internet and public directories, but no special equipment and no criminal methods. Each of the three cases above was done by a researcher or a reporter, not a criminal. The guidelines also state that unauthorised re-identification of anonymised information is an offence under Part 9B of the PDPA, which deters some intruders and not others. Leader session 2 sizes the intruder for each release route.

Build-along

Five questions before you approve a release

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Dr Hana Seow, Brightwater's research director, has promised three university partners the extract by the end of the month. Ravi says it is anonymised because the identity numbers are hashed. Lim Su-Ann has to approve it. Work through the five questions she asks, answer each one for Brightwater, and measure the fourth yourself in the widget below; then do the same for a release request at your own organisation.

Step 1 · What was removed, and how? Ask for the list, and for each one whether it was deleted or replaced. A hash or a token is a replacement: whoever holds the mapping, or can recompute the hash, can reverse it. That keeps the data pseudonymised however good the hash.

★ Step 1 · removedcolumn deleted / replaced (how) who can reverse it ________________ ___________________________ ________________ Brightwater: NRIC replaced by an unsalted hash anyone with the NRIC list

Step 2 · What was left? Every remaining column, not a summary. Brightwater's extract keeps age, sex, race, marital status, education, country of birth, workclass, occupation and income band, plus a few numeric columns.

★ Step 2 · left in the file____________________________________________________________ Brightwater: age, sex, race, marital, education, country of birth, workclass, occupation, income band, hours, capital gain and loss

Step 3 · Who receives it, under what controls? Name the recipients and what binds them: a data-sharing agreement, a ban on onward disclosure, a ban on re-identification, destruction at the end. Leader session 2 turns this into a risk register.

★ Step 3 · recipient and controlsrecipients: ______________________ how many people: ____ controls: agreement Y/N · no onward disclosure Y/N · no re-identification Y/N destroy by: __________ Brightwater: 3 university teams, about 20 researchers and students

Step 4 · What could they already know? For a realistic outsider, a colleague, a student, a reporter, list the columns they could fill in about someone in the file. Be generous: the three releases in Part 3 were undone by information nobody listed.

★ Step 4 · the outsideroutsider: ____________________ could fill in: ___________________________________________ Brightwater: a colleague knows age, sex, race, marital status, education and country of birth; perhaps workclass

Step 5 · How many are alone? Tick the columns from step 4 in the widget and read the share in a class of one. That number goes at the top of your reply to the requester, next to the word "pseudonymised".

★ Step 5 · the replyOn the ___ columns an outsider could know, ____% of rows are unique. This file is pseudonymised, not anonymised. Before release we need: ________ Brightwater: six columns, 17.18%, 5,594 people. Not approved as it stands.

Say no with a number, then a path. "Not anonymised: 17.18% are unique on six columns" is a decision a requester can act on. Follow it with the route: the Engineer track shows how coarsening and suppression take Brightwater's single-row matches from 175 of 1,000 known people to 0, and Leader session 3 covers the other tools.

Homework

Before Leader session 2

30 min
  1. Find one "anonymised" file. Pick a dataset your organisation has shared, or been asked to share, as anonymised. Ask the five questions about it.
  2. Get the number. Ask whoever prepared it for the share of rows in a class of one on the columns from question 4. If nobody can produce it, that is the finding.
  3. Read the test once. Read paragraphs 3.4 to 3.8 of PDPC's Advisory Guidelines on Selected Topics, Chapter 3, and underline the two limbs of the serious-possibility test.
  4. List the recipients. For that file, write who received it and what bound them. Leader session 2 starts from that list.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Ravi replaced every NRIC in the extract with a hash and kept every other column. Which station is the file at?

Replacing a direct identifier is the first step only. The test is whether re-identification is still a serious possibility, and on six ordinary columns 17.18% of the rows are unique.

2 · Under PDPC's guidelines, which two things does the serious-possibility test weigh?

The same file can be anonymised for one recipient under strict controls and not for the open internet. Risk lives in the data and its environment together.

3 · What did the Massachusetts, AOL and Netflix cases have in common?

A voter list, local knowledge and public reviews: in every case the outsider's information was ordinary, and the people doing it were a researcher and reporters.
