When k is not enough

Ravi's release now has k = 5: nobody in it can be singled out, and it meets the minimum PDPC's guidelines set for sharing with an external party. Part 1 · covers the homogeneity attack

Five people who look the same can share one secret

5 min live

k-anonymity hides a person among k look-alikes. If those look-alikes all have the same sensitive value, hiding among them protects nothing: the outsider does not need to know which row is their neighbour, because every row says the same thing. Machanavajjhala and colleagues named this the homogeneity attack, and paired it with a second one, the background-knowledge attack, in the paper that introduced l-diversity.

LiveTwo attacks from one paper3 min▶

Homogeneity. In the paper's example, Alice reads a 4-anonymous hospital inpatient table and knows enough about her neighbour to find his class of four. Every patient in that class has the same disease. k = 4 held; the neighbour's diagnosis leaked anyway. The paper's own summary is that k-anonymity "does not guarantee privacy against attackers using background knowledge".

Background knowledge. In the second example Alice knows Umeko, a 21-year-old Japanese woman in ZIP code 13068. Her class has more than one disease, but Alice also knows something about Umeko that rules some of them out. A class with a few values can collapse to one in the hands of someone who knows the person. Distinct values are a floor, not a guarantee.

On Brightwater's rows. At rung 4 the desk prints k 5 and l 1. Only 5 rows sit in a class where everyone shares one occupation, and none of the 1,000 people the outsider knows is among them, so the linkage attack reads 0 while l still reads 1. A different outsider, one who knows one of those five women, reads her job off the file. At rung 1, before any coarsening, 6,331 rows are in single-occupation classes.

Self-studyWhere PDPC leaves it2 min▶

PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) teaches k and stops there on purpose. Its footnote 11 says homogeneity attacks "can be addressed using the k-anonymity extensions l-diversity and t-closeness", and that these topics are outside the guide's scope. Step 5 of the same guide names attribute disclosure, learning something about a person without necessarily identifying their record, as one of the three disclosure risks a release must manage. So the guidance names the risk and points at the tools; the definitions come from the research papers below. What the PDPA requires of a release is a legal question: PDPA and DNC ↗ owns it.

Real world

A compliant file that still answers the question. Constructed Brightwater example on the real rows: Ravi Menon shows Dr Hana Seow the rung 4 release. k is 5, which meets the "minimum k-anonymity value of 5" PDPC's Advisory Guidelines (para 3.9) give for sharing with an external party. Then he opens the smallest published class: five women in their forties, white, never married, with a bachelor's degree, born outside the US, and every one of them in Prof-specialty. Hana's question is not whether a partner can find one of them. It is whether anyone who knows one of them can learn her job. The answer is yes.

Part 2 · covers l-diversity

Count the different values inside every class

5 min live

The simplest fix for homogeneity is a count. Distinct l-diversity asks that every published class contains at least l different values of the sensitive column. The release's l is the count in its least diverse class. l = 1 means at least one class is homogeneous; l = 3 means the outsider who finds a class is still left choosing between at least three values.

Livel on the ladder, and the column that cannot climb3 min▶

Occupation climbs. With occupation as the sensitive column the desk prints l 1 at rung 4, l 2 at rung 5 (k 10, 1,801 rows suppressed) and l 3 at rung 6 (20-year age bands, degree yes or no, k 10, 345 rows suppressed). Bigger classes hold more jobs, so l rises as k does, but not in lockstep: rung 5 doubles k and only reaches l 2.

Income band cannot. Income band has two values, over or under 50K. The most diverse class possible holds both, so l can never exceed 2. A rule written as "l of at least 3" is impossible to meet for this column, and a rule written as "l of at least 2" only asks that no class is entirely one band. Write the rule for the column you have, and step 2 of the build-along shows what the real rows do with it.

Read l together with its class sizes. A class of 10 with nine rows of one job and one of another has l 2, and the outsider's best guess is right nine times in ten. Distinct l counts values; it does not weigh them.

Self-studyStricter versions of l2 min▶

Distinct l is the plain count the desk uses, chosen because a reviewer can check it by eye. The TKDD paper also discusses stricter instantiations, entropy l-diversity and recursive l-diversity, which ask that the values in a class are not only different but reasonably balanced, so the nine-to-one class above would fail them. They are worth reading in the paper itself before you adopt one; this page does not quote their formal definitions. The paper to cite is Machanavajjhala et al., ACM Transactions on Knowledge Discovery from Data, vol 1, issue 1, article 3, March 2007.

Real world

Hana asks for income instead. Constructed Brightwater example on the real rows: one university partner does not want occupation at all; it wants income band, to study who earns over 50K. Dr Hana Seow asks Ravi whether the rung 6 file, which passes the desk's bar with occupation as the sensitive column (k 10, l 3, 1.06% suppressed), is just as safe with income. Ravi points out that the bar's l of 3 cannot even be asked of a two-valued column, and runs the desk again with the sensitive column switched. The build-along does the same.

Part 3 · covers t-closeness

Measure how far a class sits from the whole table

5 min live

Li, Li and Venkatasubramanian argued that counting values asks the wrong question. What an outsider learns is the difference between what the whole table says and what the class says. If 3 in 4 people in the file earn under 50K, learning that a class is all under 50K tells little; learning that a class is almost all over 50K tells a lot, even though that class has l 2. Their t-closeness asks that every class's distribution of the sensitive value sits within a distance t of the whole table's.

LiveWhat the desk computes, and why it stays high3 min▶

The paper's distance. Li and colleagues define t-closeness as the distance between a class's sensitive distribution and the whole table's being "no more than a threshold t", and they choose the Earth Mover's Distance: the minimal amount of work needed to move one distribution into the other. When every pair of categories is the same distance apart, as with job titles that have no natural order, that work is half the sum of the absolute differences in share, also called the variational distance. That is what the desk computes for every published class, and it prints the worst one.

The real numbers. With occupation sensitive, t is 0.8741 at rung 4, 0.8056 at rung 5 and 0.7116 at rung 6. Even the heaviest rung leaves a class whose job mix is far from the table's. The reason is not a bug in the release: occupation really does differ by age, sex, education and country of birth in these rows. A class of men in their forties without a degree does different work from the table as a whole.

The tension, unresolved. That difference is also exactly what Brightwater's researchers want to study. Pushing t low enough to hide it means coarsening until the classes say nothing about how jobs differ by group, which is the information the release exists to carry. No source read for this page sets a t threshold, and PDPC's guide does not teach t at all. Treat t as a measurement you report and defend, not a pass mark you can look up.

Self-study"Neither necessary nor sufficient"2 min▶

The t-closeness paper's abstract argues that l-diversity is "neither necessary nor sufficient to prevent attribute disclosure". Not sufficient: a class can hold several values and still be heavily skewed, as with the nine-to-one class, or a high-earner class in a file where most people earn less. Not necessary: a class where everyone has the majority value can have l 1 while telling the outsider almost nothing beyond what the whole table already says. That is their argument for t, and it is an argument, not a settled result: t pays for its precision by flattening the very differences analysts need. Cite Li, Li and Venkatasubramanian, 2007 IEEE 23rd International Conference on Data Engineering, pp 106-115.

Real world

Ten nurses. Constructed Brightwater example: Lim Su-Ann, Brightwater's data protection officer, reviews a separate staff survey a partner wants to release, where one published class of 10 rows is nurses and nothing else. k is 10, double PDPC's external minimum; l is 1. Her question to Ravi is whether that is fine, and the honest answer is "it depends on the table". If the survey is mostly nurses, the class teaches little and t is small. If nurses are rare in it, anyone who knows a person in that class learns their profession, and t is large. Su-Ann asks for l and t in the release record, and a sentence on which one the release relies on.

Homework

Before Engineer session 4

30 min
  1. Run the three checks on your own file. Take the dataset you sorted in Engineer session 1 and generalised in Engineer session 2. For each sensitive column, compute k, distinct l and the worst-class t, and note which class sets each one.
  2. Find your homogeneous classes. Count the rows that sit in a class with one sensitive value, as the desk does (6,331 at rung 1, 5 at rung 4 on Brightwater's rows). Decide whether each one is a disclosure or a value the whole table already makes likely.
  3. Write your two-valued rule. If any sensitive column has two values (a yes or no flag, a pass or fail), write the rule from step 4 for it, and the cost to the people who will use the file.
  4. Bring the release record lines. Engineer session 4 attacks the release with an outsider's table and asks whether the classes you checked tonight are the ones the outsider actually sees.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What is the homogeneity attack?

The outsider never needs the person's row. If every row in the class has the same value, finding the class is enough.

2 · Brightwater's rung 4 release has k = 5 and l = 1 with occupation sensitive. Why does it still leak?

k = 5 hides which row is hers; l = 1 means one class says the same thing in every row. The linkage attack read 0 because none of the 1,000 known people sat in that class, not because the class is safe.

3 · What does t measure in t-closeness?

t is about surprise: how far a class's mix sits from the table's. The desk computes it as the Earth Mover's Distance with equal distances between jobs, which is half the sum of the absolute differences in share.
