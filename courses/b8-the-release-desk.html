Part 1 · covers the ladder

Six presets, from pseudonymised to heavy

5 min live

Read the ladder top to bottom before touching a control. Each rung changes one thing. The first is what most organisations send: names gone, everything else exact. The second coarsens age. The third coarsens five columns. The fourth and fifth suppress every class under 5 and under 10. The sixth generalises harder so that fewer rows need suppressing at all.

LiveTwo numbers for risk, one for usefulness3 min▶

Single-row matches count the people the outsider can point at with certainty: their six facts lead to exactly one released row. 175 on the pseudonymised file, 0 from rung 4.

Expected re-identifications add up each person's chance of being guessed: 1 for a class of one, 0.2 for a class of five. It never reaches 0 while any of the 1,000 people are in the file, which is the honest point. Anonymisation lowers the risk; it does not delete it.

Precision kept is the usefulness side: 100% when every column is exact, 61% at rungs 3 to 5, 51% at rung 6. A risk number without a usefulness number invites a release nobody can use; show both.

Self-studyWhy rung 6 suppresses fewer rows than rung 52 min▶

Rung 5 keeps 10-year age bands and five education levels and withholds every class under 10: 1,801 rows, 5.53% of the file. Rung 6 widens age to 20-year bands and education to degree yes or no; the classes grow so much that only 345 rows, 1.06%, sit in a class under 10. Generalising harder and suppressing less is often the better trade, because suppression removes whole people and removes them unevenly: the rare combinations, the minorities and migrants, go first. Check who you withheld before you call a release representative.

Part 2 · covers the trap preset and the bar

A bench that can say no

5 min live

Two rungs fail in ways worth naming. Rung 1 is the trap: removing direct identifiers changes none of the risk numbers, because names were never in the quasi-identifiers. Rung 4 reaches k = 5 and still fails the diversity test: at least one published class has a single occupation, so anyone placed in it has their job revealed. The desk marks both, and its verdict only turns green when k, l and suppression all clear its bar.

LiveWhy rung 4's attack still finds 0 revealed jobs3 min▶

At rung 4, 5 rows sit in classes with a single occupation, and none of the outsider's 1,000 people happens to be among them, so the attack counter reads 0. The l counter reads 1 anyway, because l is computed over every published class, not over a sample. That is why both exist: the attack tells you what one realistic outsider achieves, the property tells you what the worst-placed person faces. A release review that reads only the attack sample misses the people it did not sample.

Self-studyWhere the bar comes from, and why it is contested3 min▶

There is no single correct k. PDPC's guidelines set a minimum k of 5 for sharing with external parties and 3 for internal sharing with controls (Selected Topics, para 3.9), and allow other methods that show a similar or lower risk. The research literature argues that any fixed k ignores what the outsider already knows. The desk's bar, k at least 10, l at least 3, under 5% suppressed, is a teaching threshold chosen to make the trade visible on these rows, not a standard. A public release with no controls on the recipient warrants a stricter setting than a file sent to three named researchers under contract; Leader session 2 covers how the route changes the number, and Leader session 4 how to write the number you chose and why.

Real world

Ratings with no names. In 2006 Netflix released the movie ratings of about 500,000 subscribers, names replaced by numbers, for a public prediction prize. Narayanan and Shmatikov showed that 8 ratings, 2 of which could be wrong, with dates known to within 14 days, were enough to single out 99% of the records; with IMDb reviews as the outsider's knowledge they found known users. It was the trap rung at scale: the pseudonym was never the protection, and the ratings themselves were the quasi-identifiers.

Part 3 · covers the break button

The column you forgot to declare

5 min live

Every number on the desk is computed over the columns you declared as quasi-identifiers. Publish one more column without declaring it, and k is still reported as 5, while the outsider joins on it and splits your classes again. The break button does exactly that with workclass: the file goes out with it, the k calculation never sees it.

LiveHow the forgotten column happens3 min▶

A late request. A partner asks for "just one more column" after the review. It ships without the climb being re-run.

A column that looked sensitive, not identifying. Workclass looks like an attribute of the job. A colleague knows whether you work for the government.

Two releases. The same people go out in a second file with different columns. Joined together, the two files declare more than either one. Engineer session 4 runs that attack on purpose.

The fix is procedural, not mathematical: the release record lists every published column and states for each one whether it was declared, and the desk is re-run on the final column list, not the reviewed one.

When the desk cannot get you there. If no setting clears the bar without destroying the analysis the partners need, the answer is a different tool, not a looser bar: release counts with calibrated noise (Engineer sessions 5 and 6), a synthetic file tested for copies (Engineer session 7), or access in a controlled enclave instead of a file (Leader session 2).

Homework

After the track

45 min
  1. Re-run your record cold. Close the page, reopen it, and reproduce every number on your record from the setting alone. If one differs, your record is missing a field.
  2. Swap with a colleague. Give them only your record. Can they reproduce your numbers and spot a column you forgot to declare?
  3. Try the hardest outsider. Assume the outsider also knows hours worked. Which of your settings survive? Write the sentence that says what your release does not protect against.
  4. Take it to the leaders. Leader session 4 signs off a release from exactly this record. Send yours to whoever signs at your organisation, with the break test result at the top.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 8 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the real rows, removing names and IDs (rung 1) changes the unique share and the single-row matches by how much?

Pseudonymisation removes direct identifiers. The re-identification runs through the quasi-identifiers, which are untouched.

2 · Rung 4 reaches k = 5 with 0 single-row matches. Why does the desk still not pass it?

k protects identity; l protects the value. The attack sample missed those 5 rows, the l property did not.

3 · After the break button publishes workclass undeclared, the desk still prints k = 5. What went wrong?

A property is only as complete as the column list it was computed on. Re-run the desk on the final column list.
