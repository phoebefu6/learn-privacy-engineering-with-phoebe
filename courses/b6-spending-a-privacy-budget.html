Part 1 · covers composition

Every answer spends from the same budget

5 min live

Ask two differentially private questions about the same people and the leaks add up. Dwork and Roth (2014) state it as the composition theorem: an epsilon1-differentially private mechanism and an epsilon2-differentially private mechanism, run together, are epsilon1 + epsilon2 differentially private. So a release has a total, and you decide up front how to divide it. Divide it ten ways and each answer gets one tenth of the budget, ten times the noise.

LiveWhat the composition theorem says3 min▶

Two mechanisms. Theorem 3.14 in Dwork and Roth (2014): if one mechanism is epsilon1-differentially private and another is epsilon2-differentially private, releasing both answers is epsilon1 + epsilon2 differentially private. The person in the file is protected by the sum, not by either one.

Many mechanisms. Corollary 3.15 extends it to k mechanisms: the epsilons add. Ten queries at 0.1 cost 1. Twenty queries at 0.1 cost 2. There is no free repeat, and asking the same question twice and averaging is just two queries.

What it costs in accuracy. Keep the total at 1 and split it over 10 queries: each query runs at epsilon 0.1, the Laplace scale is b = 10, and on the real counts the mean absolute error per count goes from 0.94 to 9.42. That is the trade every budget is a decision about: more questions, or sharper answers.

Self-studyA tighter bound exists, and why we stop at the sum2 min▶

The simple sum is an upper bound, and for many queries it is pessimistic. Dwork and Roth also prove an advanced composition theorem (Theorem 3.20) that gives a smaller total for many mechanisms, at the price of a small delta, the (epsilon, delta) form of the definition from Engineer session 5. It is real and widely used; it is also beyond what a release record needs to state for a handful of counting queries. Use the sum, write down that you used the sum, and leave the tighter accounting to a specialist who can show the working.

Real world

Constructed Brightwater example: the quiet second query. Ravi Menon releases the education table at epsilon 1 on Monday. On Thursday a partner asks for the same table again "with fresh noise, the first one looked odd". If Ravi runs it, the two answers together cost 2, and averaging them gets the partner closer to the true counts than either release alone. The guarantee on Monday's file did not change; the guarantee on the people did. Every re-run goes in the ledger.

Part 2 · covers post-processing and the unit of privacy

What is free, and who the guarantee is about

5 min live

Two facts make a budget usable. First, once an answer is released with differential privacy, anything you compute from it alone, rounding, clamping negatives to zero, adding bins together, costs nothing more: post-processing cannot weaken the guarantee. Second, an epsilon is a promise about some unit, one row, one event, one person per day, one person. Until that unit is named, the epsilon has no meaning a reader can check.

LivePost-processing is free3 min▶

The property. It is a well-known property of differential privacy that any computation on a released answer, which does not go back to the raw rows, keeps the same guarantee. The noise has already done its work; nothing you do to its output can recover what it hid.

What counts. Rounding to whole people. Clamping negative counts to zero (a count of -3.8 is impossible and readers stop trusting a table that shows one). Adding the 16 education counts into five broader groups. Computing shares. Drawing a chart. All free.

What does not count. Anything that touches the rows again: a fresh query, a re-run "to check", a join back to the extract. Those spend budget. And free in privacy is not free in accuracy: clamping only ever pushes counts up, so a sum of clamped counts runs high. Say which post-processing you did, so a reader knows the bias it adds.

Self-studyNIST SP 800-226 on the unit of privacy4 min▶

The unit. NIST SP 800-226, Guidelines for Evaluating Differential Privacy Guarantees (Near, Darais, Lefkovitz and Howarth, March 2025), gives the unit of privacy its own section, 2.4. Its key takeaway: user-level privacy provides stronger guarantees than attribute-level, user-day-level or event-level privacy. It also shows that guarantees with different units are not directly comparable: an event-level epsilon 1 and a user-level epsilon 1 are different promises.

Bounding contributions. When one person can contribute many events, the way to a user-level guarantee is to bound how much each person contributes before the noise is added, as the cap in the figure does. The cap costs data, which is the honest price of the stronger unit.

Epsilon is one component of many. The publication's Figure 1, "Components of a differential privacy guarantee", sets epsilon among the unit of privacy, the trust model, the query model, side channels, access control, security, data collection, exposure, algorithms and correctness, bias and utility, and says each component depends on those below it. A release record that states only epsilon has stated one component.

Real world

Constructed Brightwater example: whose budget is it? Dr Hana Seow's three university partners each want their own tables, and each asks for "a budget of 1". Lim Su-Ann, the data protection officer, asks the question that settles it: are the people in the three partners' tables different people? They are not. All three tables come from the same 32,561 rows, and the partners can share what they receive. Composition does not care who asked, so the budget belongs to the people in the file, and the three partners split one total between them.

Part 3 · covers a real budget

What a published budget looks like

5 min live

Textbook examples use epsilon around 1. On 9 June 2021 the US Census Bureau announced the privacy-loss budget for the 2020 Census redistricting data product: epsilon 19.61, made of 17.14 for the persons file and 2.47 for the housing unit data. Composition is what lets a budget be written as parts that add to a total. The size of the number is what starts the argument.

LiveReading the release honestly3 min▶

What it says. Press release CB21-CN.42 sets "a total privacy-loss budget for the redistricting data product" of epsilon 19.61, which includes 17.14 for the persons file and 2.47 for the housing unit data. 17.14 + 2.47 = 19.61: the parts compose to the total. The release names the TopDown Algorithm as the method being prepared for final testing.

What it does not say. The figure covers the redistricting data product only, not the whole 2020 Census output. The budgets for the later products are not covered here. The Bureau's use of differential privacy for 2020 is widely reported; the release itself speaks of a privacy-loss budget and the TopDown Algorithm.

The lesson for a release record. A real budget is written as a total and its parts, with the product it covers named. That is the shape of the budget line you write in the build-along.

Self-studyIs 19.61 too large? Teach the debate3 min▶

The case that it is large. The definition bounds how much any output can change when one person is added or removed by a factor of exp(epsilon). At epsilon 1 that factor is modest; at 19.61 the bound on paper is very loose. By textbook standards it is a large number, and some privacy researchers read it as a weak formal guarantee.

The case for it. Redistricting needs accurate counts for small areas, because the data is used to draw districts of equal population. A smaller epsilon would have meant more noise on exactly those small counts. The budget was chosen to balance that accuracy against the privacy loss, and a stated bound, even a loose one, is something a reader can check and argue with.

What an engineer takes from it. There is no agreed safe epsilon. The literature has not settled one and you should not pretend to. What you can do is state the number, its unit, what it covers, and why it was chosen, so the people who disagree are disagreeing about the same thing.

Real world

A budget written as parts. The 2020 Census redistricting release did not publish one epsilon; it published 19.61 and how it divides between persons and housing units. That is composition used as an accounting tool: the Bureau decided where the privacy loss would be spent before anyone saw a table. Brightwater's ledger tonight is the same idea at the scale of three university partners.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Brightwater releases one table at epsilon 0.4 and, later, another at epsilon 0.6, both about the same 32,561 people. What does the composition theorem say about the two together?

Theorem 3.14 and Corollary 3.15 in Dwork and Roth: epsilons of mechanisms run on the same people add. Different tables of the same people still compose.

2 · A partner rounds Brightwater's noisy counts, clamps the negatives to 0 and adds the 16 levels into five groups. How much more privacy budget does that cost?

Post-processing is free in privacy because it never touches the rows. It is not free in accuracy: clamping adds an upward bias, so say you did it.

3 · Two teams both report "epsilon 1". One protects each event, the other each person. Why must a release state its unit of privacy?

NIST SP 800-226 Section 2.4: user-level privacy is stronger than event-level, and guarantees with different units are not directly comparable. An epsilon without its unit cannot be read.
