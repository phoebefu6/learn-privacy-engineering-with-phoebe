Synthetic data and its leaks

"Just give them synthetic rows" sounds like the end of the privacy problem: no row is a real person, so nobody can be found. Tonight you test that sentence on 32,561 real census rows with two toy generators. One copies nothing and destroys the relationship a researcher came for. The other keeps the relationship and reproduces thousands of real people who were unique in the file. Between them sits the one measurement that tells a leak from a coincidence: the chance baseline.

Part 1 · covers what synthetic data is and its PDPC status

A generator learns from real rows, then writes new ones

5 min live

A synthetic-data generator reads the real file, learns something about it, and writes rows that look like it. Everything depends on what it learned. Learn too little and the new rows lose the patterns that made the file worth sharing. Learn too much and the new rows are the old rows with a light disguise. Tonight's two toys sit at those two ends, on the same eight columns: the six quasi-identifiers (age, sex, race, marital status, education, country of birth) plus occupation and income band.

LiveWhat PDPC says synthetic data is for3 min▶

A use case, not a technique. PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) lists seven techniques in Annex A: record suppression, character masking, pseudonymisation, generalisation, swapping, data perturbation and data aggregation. Synthetic data is not one of them. It appears on page 17 as a use case: record-level synthetic data "can be created from the original data by heavily anonymising" it.

Not for analysis. The same page says such data is "not suitable for sophisticated purposes such as AI model training or data analytics". PDPC sees it as test data: something that has the shape of the real file so a developer can build a pipeline against it, not something a researcher should draw conclusions from.

From scratch is out of scope. Footnote 7 on page 17 says generating synthetic data "from scratch" is not addressed in the guide. A model that learns from the real file and writes new rows, which is what most synthetic-data tools do, is outside what the guide covers. Treat it as a release that needs its own test, which is what the rest of tonight builds.

Self-studyThe disagreement you will meet3 min▶

Some vendors describe synthetic data as anonymous by construction: no synthetic row belongs to a real person, so there is nobody to re-identify. The argument has a real core, because a generator that learned only broad patterns writes rows nobody can be tied to. Its weak point is the word "only": nothing in the word "synthetic" says how much the generator remembered.

The research below found that, on the generators and data it tested, the privacy and the usefulness could not both be had. That is evidence about those generators, not a law about every possible one, and the field has not settled the question. The engineering norm that follows from both sides is the same: do not accept "it is synthetic" as a privacy argument, measure the file you are about to release. Whether a synthetic file is still personal data is a legal question; PDPA and DNC ↗ and GDPR ↗ own it.

Real world

Anonymisation Groundhog Day. Theresa Stadler, Bristena Oprisanu and Carmela Troncoso tested synthetic data generators against privacy attacks in "Synthetic Data - Anonymisation Groundhog Day" (arXiv 2011.07018; its text says a peer-reviewed version is published at USENIX Security 2022). Their finding: synthetic data either does not prevent inference attacks or does not retain data utility, so it offers no better tradeoff than traditional anonymisation, and where the tradeoff lands is hard to predict. Their phrase for it is "far from the holy grail" of privacy-preserving data publishing. The title is the point: the same lesson as every session before, arriving again under a new name.

Part 2 · covers utility lost

The safest generator erased the finding

5 min live

Pick one relationship a researcher would actually use and check it survives. Here it is the pay gap: the share of men earning over 50K minus the share of women. In the real rows, 30.6% of the 21,790 men and 10.9% of the 10,771 women are over 50K, a gap of 19.6 points. The independent-columns generator, which never copies a row, reports a gap of 0.2 points. The memorising generator reports 19.9.

LiveWhy the gap vanished3 min▶

Independent draws, independent columns. The first generator picks a sex from the real mix of sexes and, separately, an income band from the real mix of income bands. Nothing links the two, so in the synthetic file men and women both land near the overall share of 24.08%. Every single column looks right, and every relationship between columns is gone.

It is not only the pay gap. Education and income, age and marital status, occupation and sex: any pattern that lives between two columns is erased the same way. A researcher running a regression on this file gets clean-looking coefficients near zero and a wrong paper.

The memorising generator keeps it for the worst reason. Its 19.9 is close to the real 19.6 because its rows are, mostly, the real rows. The relationship survives because the people survive.

Self-studyChoosing the utility check3 min▶

A utility check is only useful if it tests what the recipient will actually do. Ask the requester for the one or two analyses they plan, then compute the same statistic on the real rows and on the synthetic rows. Matching column histograms is the weakest possible check; the independent-columns toy passes it perfectly and fails every analysis that matters.

Pick relationships across columns, and include at least one that involves a small group, because generators tend to smooth small groups away first. Write the real value, the synthetic value and the difference in the release record. A file that fails the utility check is not a safe file that happens to be inconvenient: it is a file that will produce wrong findings with Brightwater's name on the data source.

Constructed Brightwater example

The paper that found no gap. One of Brightwater's three university teams studies earnings by sex. Ravi Menon, the data engineer, offers them an independent-columns synthetic file as the quick safe option. Dr Hana Seow, the research director, runs the team's headline statistic on it first: 0.2 points instead of 19.6. The file would have been safe and the team's paper would have been wrong. The fix is not a better-looking synthetic file; it is a different route, such as generalised row-level data under agreement, which Engineer sessions 2 to 4 measured.

Part 3 · covers memorised rows and the chance baseline

An exact match is not a leak until it beats chance

5 min live

The obvious privacy test is to count synthetic rows that are identical to a real row. Run it on the generator that copies nothing on purpose and the answer is not zero: 8,855 of 32,561 synthetic rows (27.2%) match some real row on all eight columns, and 2,183 of the 13,496 real people who are unique on those eight columns are reproduced exactly. All of that is chance. The memorising generator matches 26,987 rows (82.88%) and reproduces 6,818 unique people. The leak is the difference: 6,818 minus 2,183 is 4,635 unique people the generator remembered.

LiveWhy chance makes copies3 min▶

Common combinations are easy to hit. Most real rows sit in crowded combinations: white, US-born, high-school graduate, a common occupation. Draw each column at random from its real mix and you land on one of those crowded combinations often. That is why 27.2% of the independent file matches a real row without the generator remembering anyone.

Even rare combinations get hit. A person unique on eight columns has a combination no one else shares, but it is built from values that are each common enough. With 32,561 random draws, 2,183 of the 13,496 unique combinations come up by chance.

So measure against a baseline. Run a generator that cannot have memorised anyone, on the same columns and the same number of rows, and treat its counts as the floor. A real generator's counts above that floor are what it learned about specific people. The excess here is 4,635 unique people, roughly a third of everyone who was unique.

Self-studyThree attacks, not one3 min▶

Stadler et al. evaluate synthetic data against three kinds of attack, and an exact-match count touches only the first. Linkage: can an outsider tie a synthetic row back to a real person, the attack of Engineer session 4. Membership inference: can an attacker tell whether a particular person was in the training data at all, which is itself a disclosure when the file is "people treated at this clinic". Attribute inference: knowing some of a person's columns, can an attacker guess a sensitive one better from the synthetic file than without it.

A generator can pass the exact-match test and still fail the other two, for example by writing near-copies with one column changed. The memorising toy does exactly that with age, and in this count its jittered copies already slip past an exact test. Treat exact matches as the cheap first screen, not the verdict.

Constructed Brightwater example

"Realistic rows" for the hackathon. A partner university is running a student hackathon and asks Brightwater for "realistic rows" from the census extract. Ravi trains a generator and reports that 80-odd percent of its rows match a real row, which alarms Lim Su-Ann, the DPO. Before anyone decides, Ravi runs the independent-columns baseline: if the baseline also matched around a quarter of rows, then the excess, not the raw count, is the number Su-Ann needs. For a hackathon, PDPC's own framing fits: test data with the shape of the file, which is exactly what the low-utility baseline file is good for.

Test a synthetic file before release

Lim Su-Ann will not sign a synthetic release on the word "synthetic". She wants four numbers, each next to its baseline, and a decision. Step 4 · The first test in pandas. Run this on your own machine next to the public file (archive.ics.uci.edu, dataset 2, adult.zip). It builds an independent-columns file with numpy's seeded generator and counts how many synthetic rows are identical to a real row on the eight columns. The output below is pasted from a real run (pandas 2.1.4, numpy 1.26.4, seed 7). Its numbers differ from the widget's because the random generator differs; they land within about 120 rows of the widget's 8,855 copies and within a handful of its 2,183 unique people.

★ Step 4 · pandas, seededimport numpy as np import pandas as pd cols = ["age", "workclass", "fnlwgt", "education", "education_num", "marital", "occupation", "relationship", "race", "sex", "capital_gain", "capital_loss", "hours", "country", "income"] df = pd.read_csv("adult.data", names=cols, skipinitialspace=True) keep = ["age", "sex", "race", "marital", "education", "country", "occupation", "income"] real = df[keep] # independent columns: every column drawn on its own from its real values rng = np.random.default_rng(7) n = len(real) syn = pd.DataFrame({c: real[c].to_numpy()[rng.integers(0, n, n)] for c in keep}) real_counts = real.value_counts() hits = syn.merge(real_counts.rename("n_real").reset_index(), on=keep, how="left") copies = hits["n_real"].notna().sum() unique_real = real_counts[real_counts == 1].index syn_keys = set(map(tuple, syn.to_numpy())) reproduced = sum(1 for k in unique_real if k in syn_keys) print(f"synthetic rows identical to a real row: {copies:,} ({100 * copies / n:.2f}%)") print(f"unique real rows reproduced: {reproduced:,} of {len(unique_real):,}") # output of a real run: # synthetic rows identical to a real row: 8,733 (26.82%) # unique real rows reproduced: 2,185 of 13,496

Read the pattern, not the digits. Both runs agree that roughly a quarter of a copy-nothing file matches real rows and that about 2,200 unique people come back by chance; and both find the same 13,496 unique people in the real file, because that part involves no randomness. To test a real generator, load its output as syn and run the same lines; then run this baseline next to it and report the difference.

Keep the baseline script. The baseline costs nothing to run and changes with the columns, so re-run it whenever the column list or the row count changes. A synthetic count reported without its baseline is a number nobody can interpret, in either direction.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Engineer session 7 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does PDPC's Guide to Basic Anonymisation (2022) say about record-level synthetic data made by heavy anonymisation?

PDPC treats synthetic data as a use case on page 17, not an Annex A technique, and says it is not suitable for AI training or analytics. Generating it from scratch is outside the guide (footnote 7).

2 · The independent-columns generator copies no row on purpose, yet reproduces 2,183 of 13,496 unique real people exactly. What does that tell you about an exact-match test?

Chance alone produces 2,183. Against that baseline, the memorising generator's 6,818 means 4,635 people were remembered; without the baseline neither number can be read.

3 · The real pay gap is 19.6 points. The independent-columns file shows 0.2 and the memorising file shows 19.9. What does this show?

Drawing columns independently erases every relationship between them. The memorising file keeps the gap because its rows are mostly the real rows, which is the privacy failure from Part 3.
